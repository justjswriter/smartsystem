\chapter{Theoretical Part}

\section{Literature Review}

\subsection{Smart Plant Monitoring Systems}

Smart plant monitoring systems are part of the broader transformation of agriculture into a data-driven and technology-supported domain. In the literature, smart agriculture is generally described as an approach that integrates sensors, Internet of Things devices, data processing tools, communication technologies, and decision-support methods to improve agricultural monitoring and resource management. This shift changes plant care from a mainly manual and experience-based process into a system where decisions can be supported by continuous environmental data. Existing reviews emphasize that smart agriculture is not limited to one technology, but is based on the integration of sensing, connectivity, analytics, and user-oriented software platforms \cite{ahmed2024smart,sophocleous2017precision,sharma2024integrating}.

The need for plant monitoring systems is connected with the limitations of traditional observation. Manual inspection depends on the user's attention, experience, and frequency of observation. It may be sufficient for visible plant problems, but it does not provide continuous information about environmental conditions. Huda et al. note that traditional plant monitoring methods require repeated human inspection and may miss early problems because they do not provide continuous quantitative data about environmental parameters \cite{huda2024iot}. This is especially important for monitoring soil moisture, air temperature, air humidity, and light intensity in order to identify abnormal environmental values before visible plant damage occurs.

A smart plant monitoring system usually consists of two connected parts: the physical monitoring layer and the software layer. The physical layer includes sensors and modules that collect environmental data. The software layer stores, processes, analyzes, and visualizes the collected data for the user. In IoT-enabled plant monitoring, sensor modules can measure environmental parameters and transmit them to a cloud or backend platform, where the data become available through a mobile or web interface \cite{huda2024iot}. This structure is important because raw sensor data alone are not enough; users need an understandable interface, alerts, and interpretation of the current condition.

The literature describes smart plant monitoring as a practical application of IoT and wireless sensor networks. Mowla et al. explain that IoT and WSN technologies are used in smart agriculture for irrigation, soil moisture monitoring, fertilizer optimization, pest and disease monitoring, energy management, and real-time field data collection \cite{mowla2023iot}. Although not all these functions are included in every system, they demonstrate that plant monitoring is usually connected with a wider technological ecosystem. The present project adopts this idea in a narrower form by focusing on environmental monitoring, dashboard visualization, alerts, and AI-supported recommendations.

Plant monitoring systems may differ significantly depending on their target users and deployment context. Some systems are designed for large agricultural fields, where the main goal is to increase productivity, optimize irrigation, or support crop protection. Other systems are intended for greenhouse environments, where the monitored parameters can be more controlled and the system may include actuators. A third group includes smaller systems for potted plants, plant owners, educational laboratories, or small greenhouses. The literature indicates that accessibility, cost, and usability remain important issues for smart agriculture adoption, especially in resource-constrained or small-scale environments \cite{miller2025iot,nawaz2025iot}.

For small-scale users, the value of a monitoring system is not only in the sensor measurements, but also in how clearly the system explains the plant condition. A plant owner may not interpret raw numerical values correctly without additional support. Therefore, smart plant monitoring systems should connect data collection with understandable decision support. Existing literature indicates that data analytics and AI methods can contribute to decision-making in agriculture by processing sensor data and identifying patterns or abnormal conditions \cite{mansoor2025integration,miller2025iot}. At the same time, these systems must remain understandable and practical for users.

A smart plant monitoring system can be considered useful when it supports several basic functions: data collection, data storage, data visualization, condition assessment, and user notification. Huda et al. present a plant monitoring system that includes environmental sensors, cloud-based data handling, a user application, and secure access mechanisms \cite{huda2024iot}. This example shows that a monitoring system should not be understood as a sensor alone. It is an integrated software-hardware solution where the value is created through the combination of data acquisition, storage, interpretation, and user interaction.

In the context of this diploma project, the smart plant monitoring system is defined as a web-based AI-IoT monitoring platform. Its purpose is to collect environmental data from IoT sensors, store measurements in a database, visualize current and historical values, assess plant condition, generate alerts, and provide plant care recommendations. This definition follows the general direction of smart agriculture literature, but the scope is intentionally limited to a functional web-based system suitable for plant owners, small greenhouses, and educational smart agriculture scenarios.

The literature also shows that smart agriculture systems may face practical limitations. These limitations include high implementation cost, connectivity issues, data quality problems, sensor reliability, and the need for technical expertise \cite{miller2025iot,nawaz2025iot}. These limitations are relevant to the proposed system because they justify a restrained and transparent project scope. The system should not be presented as a complete replacement for professional agronomic expertise or as a universal diagnostic tool. Instead, it should be positioned as a decision-support system based on environmental monitoring.

Therefore, smart plant monitoring systems provide the theoretical and practical basis for the proposed project. They demonstrate how IoT sensors and software systems can support plant care by collecting environmental data and presenting it to users in a more accessible form. At the same time, the literature suggests that the effectiveness of such systems depends on careful system design, reliable data handling, transparent analysis, and realistic limitations \cite{mowla2023iot,miller2025iot,nawaz2025iot}.
\subsection{IoT Technologies in Plant Condition Monitoring}

The Internet of Things is one of the main technological foundations of plant condition monitoring. In smart agriculture, IoT connects physical monitoring devices, communication infrastructure, data storage components, and software services into a single monitoring environment. Mowla et al. describe IoT and wireless sensor networks as important technologies for smart agriculture because they support remote monitoring, real-time data collection, and data-driven decision-making across different agricultural applications \cite{mowla2023iot,ayaz2019iot}. This makes IoT relevant to plant condition monitoring, where environmental data must be collected and processed systematically.

A typical IoT-based monitoring system in smart agriculture can be described as a layered data flow from physical data collection to digital monitoring. At the sensing level, sensor nodes collect measurements from the monitored environment. At the communication level, the collected data are transmitted to a gateway, server, or cloud platform. At the processing level, the received data are stored and prepared for further use. Depending on the system design, the processed data can then be used for remote monitoring, analysis, or decision support. This layered structure is consistent with IoT-WSN architectures and smart agriculture monitoring models discussed in the literature \cite{mowla2023iot,qazi2022iot}.

Wireless sensor networks are often discussed together with IoT because they provide the sensing and communication layer for agricultural monitoring. WSNs consist of distributed sensor nodes that collect data from the environment and transmit them through wireless communication channels. In smart agriculture, WSNs can support soil moisture monitoring, irrigation control, disease and pest monitoring, energy consumption monitoring, and other tasks \cite{mowla2023iot}. The same general principle is relevant for plant condition monitoring because environmental indicators must be obtained from the physical growing environment before they can be analyzed by software.

Communication technologies are important because agricultural monitoring depends on reliable data transmission. Existing literature discusses several wireless communication options used in smart agriculture, including Wi-Fi, ZigBee, SigFox, LoRaWAN, and other network protocols \cite{mowla2023iot}. These technologies differ in communication range, energy consumption, bandwidth, cost, and deployment complexity. For example, low-power wide-area technologies may be useful for large fields or remote areas, while short-range communication approaches may be sufficient for indoor plants, greenhouse environments, or educational prototypes.

IoT monitoring systems often require a balance between data frequency and energy consumption. Frequent data transmission can provide more detailed monitoring, but it may increase power usage and network load. Tsapparellas et al. discuss this issue in the context of low-cost and low-power IoT systems, where data volume and transmission frequency can be constrained by communication protocols such as LoRaWAN \cite{tsapparellas2020laplacian}. This is relevant for plant monitoring because practical systems should collect enough data for useful analysis without assuming unlimited hardware, energy, or network resources.

Cloud platforms and backend services are commonly used to store and process IoT data. Huda et al. describe a plant monitoring system in which sensor data are transmitted to a cloud platform and accessed through a mobile application \cite{huda2024iot}. This type of design shows that IoT-based plant monitoring is not limited to sensor measurement alone; collected data must also be stored, processed, and made accessible through a software interface. Similar integration of IoT, soil databases, and AI-supported backend design is also discussed by Kalantzopoulos et al. \cite{kalantzopoulos2024wesis}.

Security is also a relevant aspect of IoT-based plant monitoring. Huda et al. emphasize secure authentication mechanisms such as two-factor authentication and JSON Web Tokens in their IoT-enabled plant monitoring system \cite{huda2024iot}. This indicates that IoT monitoring systems should consider not only data collection, but also controlled access to collected data, especially when sensor data are connected to user accounts or remote applications.

Real-time monitoring can be useful when new sensor readings or abnormal environmental values need to be displayed in the software interface without long delays. Smart agriculture reviews identify real-time monitoring as one of the main advantages of IoT-based systems \cite{ahmed2024smart,mowla2023iot}. However, the exact implementation of real-time updates depends on the technical scope, communication method, and system architecture.

The literature also identifies several challenges in agricultural IoT deployment. These include connectivity limitations, infrastructure costs, energy constraints, data security issues, interoperability problems, and limited technical expertise among users \cite{miller2025iot,nawaz2025iot}. These challenges become especially important when systems are intended for small-scale or resource-constrained users. Therefore, plant monitoring systems should be designed based on realistic assumptions about cost, communication reliability, data quality, and user accessibility.

In plant condition monitoring, IoT should be understood as the data collection and transmission foundation rather than the final goal of the system. The value of IoT data appears when measurements can be stored, interpreted, visualized, and used to support decisions. This view is consistent with the general direction of smart agriculture systems described in the literature, where IoT is combined with data processing and decision-support methods to improve monitoring and management \cite{mowla2023iot,qazi2022iot,ahmed2024smart}.

Thus, IoT technologies provide the technical basis for continuous plant condition monitoring. By converting physical measurements into digital records, these systems transform environmental variables into usable information. At the same time, existing sources show that IoT systems should be developed with practical constraints in mind, including cost, connectivity, energy consumption, data quality, security, and user accessibility \cite{miller2025iot,nawaz2025iot}.

\subsection{Sensors for Environmental Data Collection}

Sensors are the primary data source in plant condition monitoring systems. They measure environmental conditions and convert them into digital values that can be stored, processed, and analyzed by software. In smart agriculture, sensor data may describe soil conditions, atmospheric conditions, light environment, nutrient status, and plant physiological responses \cite{huda2024iot,mowla2023iot,reza2025trends}.

For the proposed system, the selected environmental parameters are soil moisture, air temperature, air humidity, and light intensity. This set is suitable for a functional diploma prototype because it focuses on basic environmental monitoring while avoiding unnecessary hardware complexity. Huda et al. describe temperature, humidity, and soil moisture as key environmental parameters in an IoT-enabled plant monitoring system \cite{huda2024iot}, while broader smart agriculture literature also recognizes light environment as an important group of monitored indicators \cite{mowla2023iot}.

Soil moisture is especially relevant because water management is one of the most common applications of smart agriculture. IoT-based systems frequently use soil moisture monitoring to support irrigation-related decisions and identify under-watering or over-watering conditions \cite{mowla2023iot,hassan2021systematic}. Gaitan et al. also use soil moisture together with temperature and humidity in an automated irrigation system with AI-supported recommendations \cite{gaitan2025integrating}.

The number and type of sensors directly affect the cost, complexity, and maintainability of a monitoring system. Tsapparellas et al. studied feature reduction in IoT systems for agricultural monitoring and decision-support, showing that reducing the number of sensor types can be useful for low-cost and low-power designs \cite{tsapparellas2020laplacian}. This supports the decision to use a limited set of environmental indicators instead of attempting to monitor every possible agricultural parameter.

More advanced systems may include additional sensors and data sources, such as pH, nutrient concentration, water level, turbidity, NPK sensors, cameras, or CO2 sensors. For example, Raju et al. describe a hydroponics system that uses several sensors and a camera module for nutrient prediction and plant disease detection \cite{raju2022hydroponics}. Ali et al. include soil moisture, CO2, temperature, and camera sensors in an AI-powered IoT system for plant monitoring and actuation \cite{ali2024automated}. Lin et al. also show that greenhouse monitoring can include specialized sensing for disease-related observation \cite{lin2025spore}. These examples show broader possibilities of smart monitoring, but they also require higher hardware and algorithmic complexity.

The proposed system does not include image-based sensing, nutrient solution monitoring, or actuator-based control because its scope is environmental condition monitoring and recommendation support for small-scale users. This distinction is important because image-based disease detection and hydroponic control require different datasets, sensors, models, and validation methods. Siddiquee et al. use computer vision and CNN-based methods for crop detection and classification tasks \cite{siddiquee2022algorithms}, which are valuable but outside the scope of the current project.

Sensor data can also be influenced by calibration, sensor quality, placement, and environmental variability, which may affect measurement results. Reviews on smart sensing technologies identify data quality, sensor reliability, interoperability, and real-world implementation gaps as important issues \cite{miller2025iot}. Therefore, the proposed system uses sensor data for decision support, but does not present them as sufficient evidence for disease diagnosis, guaranteed plant recovery, or professional agronomic assessment \cite{miller2025iot,nawaz2025iot}.
\subsection{Artificial Intelligence in Plant Condition Analysis}

Artificial intelligence in agriculture is increasingly used to analyze sensor data, identify patterns, and support decision-making. In smart agriculture, AI is often combined with IoT because sensor-based systems collect data about the environment and system performance that need to be analyzed. Reviews on AI and IoT in agriculture describe AI as a tool for extracting useful information from sensor data and supporting more informed decisions in farming and plant management \cite{miller2025iot,mansoor2025integration,alzubi2023artificial}.

AI applications in agriculture are diverse. These applications include disease detection, pest identification, irrigation planning, yield forecasting, crop monitoring, image-based analysis, and environmental risk assessment. AI and the Internet of Things can jointly support smart agriculture by improving forecasting, optimizing resource management, and supporting farm management decisions \cite{qazi2022iot}. Hassan et al. also analyze how advanced monitoring and control strategies, including IoT, machine learning, artificial intelligence, and sensor-based technologies, are used to address agricultural problems such as irrigation, plant disease monitoring, and stress detection \cite{hassan2021systematic}. Indira et al. also describe an agricultural monitoring system that combines IoT and AI in an integrated monitoring setup \cite{indira2023fabrication}.

In plant condition monitoring, AI can support the interpretation of environmental sensor values. A single sensor reading may not be sufficient to determine plant condition because environmental parameters are often interdependent. For example, the interpretation of soil moisture may depend on accompanying atmospheric conditions such as air temperature and humidity. Machine learning methods can process multiple input features together and classify a condition into predefined categories. This makes AI useful as a decision-support layer in sensor-based monitoring systems.

Different AI methods are used depending on the type of agricultural data and the target task. Structured sensor data can be processed using traditional machine learning methods, while image-based tasks often rely on computer vision and deep learning. Reviews of smart sensing technologies in agriculture mention different machine learning models, including random forests, support vector machines, convolutional neural networks, and other approaches depending on the data type and application area \cite{miller2025iot}. Lightweight deployment is also discussed in TinyML-enabled IoT systems \cite{helene2025tinyml}. This indicates that the choice of AI method should correspond to the available data and the purpose of analysis.

Rule-based logic also remains relevant in plant condition analysis because it can provide transparent explanations for system decisions. In practical monitoring scenarios, users may need to understand why a certain condition is identified as normal, requiring attention, or critical. Rule-based assessment can connect abnormal environmental values with interpretable risk factors and recommendations. This is especially important because smart agriculture literature identifies transparency, reliability, and user understanding as important challenges for AI-based systems \cite{miller2025iot,nawaz2025iot}.

A hybrid approach can combine rule-based interpretation with machine learning classification. In such an approach, rule-based logic provides explainable baseline assessment, while machine learning can add pattern-based classification support. This combination is useful when a monitoring system needs to remain understandable for users while also incorporating the analytical capabilities of artificial intelligence. Existing work indicates that AI can support decision-making in agriculture, but practical limitations such as data quality, deployment context, and interpretability must also be considered \cite{mansoor2025integration,miller2025iot,nawaz2025iot}.

It is important to distinguish sensor-based condition classification from image-based disease detection. Several existing systems use CNNs or image processing methods for plant disease recognition, fruit detection, or crop classification. Siddiquee et al. use CNN and image processing methods for tomato monitoring tasks such as detection, quantification, ripeness checking, and infected vegetable detection \cite{siddiquee2022algorithms}. Raju et al. use deep learning in a smart hydroponics system for nutrient prediction and plant disease detection \cite{raju2022hydroponics}. Ibrahim et al. also present an AI-IoT system focused on plant disease detection and treatment \cite{ibrahim2025pivot}. These approaches are valuable, but they require image datasets, model training procedures, and validation methods that differ from tabular sensor-based analysis.

Recommendations made with the help of AI should also be used with caution. The literature indicates that AI can support decision-making; however, challenges such as data availability, transparency, deployment cost, connectivity, and reliability should also be considered \cite{miller2025iot,nawaz2025iot}. Therefore, AI-based analysis in plant monitoring should be understood as decision support rather than as guaranteed biological diagnosis or proof of plant recovery.

The assessment of models used for plant monitoring with AI techniques should always be understood within the context of the data used for training and testing. In cases where the model is trained with controlled or manually labeled data, its performance can be measured based on those predefined conditions, but it does not necessarily reflect its accuracy in real plant cultivation scenarios. This is important because real deployments may involve environmental variability, sensor errors, incomplete data, and differences between plants or growing conditions \cite{miller2025iot,nawaz2025iot}. Therefore, the evaluation of such a model should be presented as validation of the software and AI module within the project scope, rather than as agronomic or biological proof.

In this context, artificial intelligence should be understood as an analytical support layer for plant condition monitoring. It can help interpret sensor data, classify environmental states, and support user decisions. However, AI-based agricultural systems should be designed with clear assumptions, realistic validation, and explicit limitations. This is especially important when a system relies on environmental sensor data rather than direct biological diagnosis or image-based disease detection \cite{qazi2022iot,miller2025iot,nawaz2025iot}.
\subsection{Existing Plant Monitoring Solutions}

Existing plant monitoring solutions can be grouped into several categories according to their purpose and scale. Some systems are customized for individual plants or small-scale environments, whereas others focus on greenhouse monitoring, smart irrigation, hydroponics, disease prediction, crop monitoring, or automation. The available literature shows that AI-IoT applications are widely used in agriculture, although the capabilities and complexity of such systems differ depending on the target application \cite{ahmed2024smart,mowla2023iot,wang2022development,luo2025formation}.

One relevant example of an IoT-enabled plant monitoring system is the system described by Huda et al. The system monitors environmental parameters, sends data to a cloud platform, and provides user access through a mobile application \cite{huda2024iot}. The study also addresses power optimization and secure authentication as important practical aspects of IoT monitoring. This type of system is relevant to the present project because it focuses on environmental monitoring and user access to plant data.

Another direction is automated irrigation. Gaitan et al. present an automated irrigation system that collects temperature, humidity, and soil moisture data and uses artificial intelligence to provide climate-related suggestions and reports \cite{gaitan2025integrating}. This system demonstrates the application of environmental sensor data in water management and decision-making. However, the main purpose of the system is to improve irrigation practices, while the present project focuses on plant condition monitoring, alerting, dashboard visualization, and recommendation support.

Hydroponic monitoring systems represent a more specialized category. Raju et al. describe an IoT-based AI controller with a mobile application for smart hydroponics farming. Their system includes sensors such as NPK, sunlight, turbidity, pH, temperature, water level, and a camera module, and it applies deep learning for nutrient level prediction and plant disease detection \cite{raju2022hydroponics}. This solution is technically advanced, but it targets hydroponic farming rather than general plant owners or small greenhouses with basic environmental monitoring needs.

Image-based crop monitoring systems form another important category. Siddiquee et al. propose an IoT-based smart agriculture monitoring system with algorithms for vegetable detection, quantification, ripeness checking, and infected vegetable detection \cite{siddiquee2022algorithms}. Their work includes computer vision and CNN-based approaches. Such systems demonstrate the potential of AI in agriculture, but they require image data, training datasets, and a different validation process from sensor-based environmental monitoring.

AI-powered IoT systems may also include actuation. Ali et al. develop a system for chili plant monitoring that uses sensors, image processing, noise elimination, and actuators such as pumps and fans \cite{ali2024automated}. The system can respond to detected conditions by activating hardware components. This approach is more automated than the proposed diploma system. In contrast, the proposed system does not include actuator control and is limited to monitoring, alerting, and recommendation support.

The literature also includes systems and reviews focused on wireless sensor networks and feature reduction. Tsapparellas et al. study an IoT system for plant monitoring and use Laplacian scores to reduce sensor features while preserving decision-support usefulness \cite{tsapparellas2020laplacian}. This is relevant because it supports the idea that a monitoring system can be designed with a limited set of meaningful sensors rather than a large number of parameters. For a functional diploma prototype, this approach is practical and cost-aware.

A common pattern across existing solutions is that many systems address a specific agricultural task. Some focus on irrigation, others on hydroponics, crop disease detection, fruit counting, pest detection, or greenhouse control. This specialization is useful, but it can also create a gap for integrated yet accessible plant monitoring systems. The proposed project is positioned in this gap by combining plant profiles, IoT sensor data, dashboard visualization, condition assessment, alert lifecycle management, and hybrid AI-supported recommendations within one web application.

Another important observation is that some existing systems are primarily hardware-centered or algorithm-centered. Hardware-centered systems may demonstrate sensor and communication modules but provide limited software architecture. Algorithm-centered systems may report AI model performance but may not include complete user management, alert lifecycle, or dashboard functionality. The proposed system is positioned as a functional diploma prototype, so its value lies in integrating backend, database, frontend, IoT data ingestion, alerts, recommendations, and AI support into one coherent system.

Therefore, existing plant monitoring solutions provide a useful technical background for the proposed project. They confirm the relevance of IoT sensors, environmental monitoring, AI-supported decision-making, and user applications. At the same time, they show why the proposed system should have a clearly limited and realistic scope: environmental condition monitoring and decision support rather than disease diagnosis, industrial automation, or full greenhouse control \cite{huda2024iot,gaitan2025integrating,raju2022hydroponics,siddiquee2022algorithms,ali2024automated}.
\section{Comparative Analysis of Existing Systems}

\subsection{Consumer Plant Monitoring Devices}

Consumer plant monitoring devices are usually designed for individual users who want to simplify everyday plant care. Such devices may measure basic environmental characteristics such as soil moisture content, temperature, air humidity, and light intensity. The main aim here is to provide the user with clear data, such as whether a plant needs to be watered or whether current light levels are adequate. This user-oriented direction is consistent with the general need for accessible plant monitoring tools described in the literature \cite{huda2024iot}.

The main advantage of consumer plant monitoring devices is simplicity. They are usually easier to install and use than industrial smart agriculture systems. For plant owners, a simple device that provides basic measurements may already be useful because it reduces dependence on manual observation. Huda et al. note that manual plant monitoring may miss early problems and does not provide continuous quantitative data \cite{huda2024iot}. Consumer devices respond to this problem by making environmental data available to users.

However, consumer plant monitoring devices often have limited functionality. They may show current sensor values but may not provide advanced data storage, structured dashboard history, multiple plant profiles, alert lifecycle management, role-based access control, or transparent AI-supported condition classification. From a software engineering perspective, this limits their usefulness as integrated monitoring platforms. Smart agriculture literature indicates that monitoring becomes more valuable when sensing is combined with data processing, visualization, and decision support \cite{ahmed2024smart,mowla2023iot}.

Consumer plant monitoring devices may also be limited in multi-user and administrative functionality. A typical individual device is not designed for admin monitoring of users, sensors, alerts, and logs. In contrast, the proposed system includes User and Admin roles, which allows the system to support both plant owners and administrative monitoring. This requirement is consistent with the idea that IoT systems require secure user access and structured management of data \cite{huda2024iot}.

Compared with consumer devices, the proposed system is broader on the software side. It is not only a measurement tool, but an integrated web application. It links plants, sensors, sensor readings, condition analysis, alerts, recommendations, and administrative monitoring. At the same time, the system has a narrower scope than industrial smart agriculture platforms because it does not include actuator control, disease diagnosis, or large-scale farm automation. This scope is appropriate for a software prototype designed for plant owners, small greenhouses, and educational scenarios.

Therefore, consumer plant monitoring devices confirm that there is a practical need for accessible plant care tools. However, these limitations indicate the need for an integrated web-based system that combines basic environmental monitoring with dashboard visualization, alert lifecycle management, and hybrid AI-supported analysis.
\subsection{Greenhouse Monitoring and Smart Irrigation Platforms}

Greenhouse monitoring and smart irrigation platforms represent a more advanced category of plant monitoring systems. In general, these platforms are developed to monitor environmental conditions and support decisions concerning watering, climate, and plant growth. Compared with consumer devices, these platforms often include more sensors, more complex data processing logic, and sometimes automated control functions. The literature describes irrigation management as one of the major application areas of IoT and wireless sensor networks in smart agriculture \cite{mowla2023iot,hassan2021systematic}.

Smart irrigation systems use available environmental data to support more efficient management of water resources. Soil moisture is one of the most important parameters for such systems, as it helps to assess the need for irrigation. Gaitan et al. describe an automated irrigation system that collects soil moisture, temperature, and humidity data and applies AI-supported analysis to generate recommendations \cite{gaitan2025integrating}. However, irrigation-centered systems are usually focused on water management rather than the full plant monitoring workflow.

Greenhouse monitoring platforms may include additional functions such as climate monitoring, nutrient management, lighting management, and sometimes disease and pest monitoring. In controlled environments, sensor data can be used not only for observation but also for automated actions. Raju et al. present a smart hydroponics system with IoT sensors, a mobile application, and AI-based components for nutrient prediction and disease detection \cite{raju2022hydroponics}. This type of platform demonstrates the potential of advanced AI-IoT integration, but it is more complex than the proposed system.

The main advantage of greenhouse and irrigation platforms is their practical value for controlled cultivation. They can help reduce water consumption, stabilize conditions, and ensure more consistent monitoring. Smart agriculture reviews show that Internet of Things technologies can contribute to resource management, irrigation, and real-time monitoring \cite{ahmed2024smart,mowla2023iot}. These advantages explain why such systems are widely studied in the literature.

However, these platforms may be too complex or too specialized for small-scale plant owners or educational users. Hydroponic and greenhouse systems often require specific sensors, actuators, cultivation methods, and installation conditions. Additional technical skills may also be needed for configuration and maintenance. Nawaz and Babar emphasize that resource-constrained environments require practical, affordable, and accessible solutions \cite{nawaz2025iot}. This supports the focus of the current project on a functional software solution rather than on a complete greenhouse automation system.

Another limitation is that greenhouse and irrigation systems often focus on physical control actions. They may activate irrigation, ventilation, or other actuators. The proposed system does not include actuator control. Instead, it generates alerts and recommendations so that users can decide what action to take. This distinction is important because it keeps the system within a software engineering scope and avoids claims about complete automation.

Compared with greenhouse and irrigation platforms, the proposed system is less focused on automated control and more focused on integrated monitoring and decision support. It includes plant profiles, IoT sensor data storage, dashboard visualization, hybrid AI-supported classification, recommendations, and alert lifecycle management. This makes it suitable for educational and small-scale contexts where the objective is to understand and monitor plant condition rather than automatically control the entire environment.

Thus, greenhouse monitoring and smart irrigation platforms provide important reference points for the proposed project. They demonstrate the value of IoT data, environmental monitoring, and AI-supported recommendations. At the same time, their complexity and specialization justify a more focused system scope for the present diploma project \cite{gaitan2025integrating,raju2022hydroponics,nawaz2025iot}.
\subsection{Limitations of Existing Solutions}

Existing smart plant monitoring and smart agriculture solutions face several limitations that are repeatedly identified in the literature. The first limitation is implementation cost. Sensors, communication modules, cloud infrastructure, maintenance, and technical setup can make smart agriculture systems expensive. Miller et al. identify infrastructure costs as one of the problems limiting the implementation of smart sensing technologies \cite{miller2025iot}. Nawaz and Babar also emphasize that low-cost and practical solutions are required in resource-constrained environments \cite{nawaz2025iot}.

The second limitation is connectivity. IoT systems depend on data transfer between sensors and software platforms. In agricultural conditions, connection quality can be affected by distance, power availability, network coverage, and the selected communication protocol. Mowla et al. discuss communication protocols and wireless network issues as important parts of IoT-WSN systems for smart agriculture \cite{mowla2023iot}. Although the proposed system is not designed for large rural deployment, connectivity remains important because sensor data must reliably reach the backend.

The third limitation is data quality. Sensor readings may be abnormal, incomplete, delayed, or affected by calibration and placement. Miller et al. identify data quality and real-world implementation gaps as important challenges for smart sensing technologies \cite{miller2025iot}. This limitation is critical for AI-supported systems because the quality of predictions depends on the quality of input data. Therefore, the proposed system should include validation and should present AI results as support rather than as a final agronomic assessment.

The fourth limitation is interoperability. In a smart agriculture system, different devices, data formats, communication protocols, and platforms can be used. Differences between components may cause integration issues, especially when sensors from different vendors are combined or when the system is extended later. Interoperability is identified as a major constraint in research on IoT and AI technologies in agriculture \cite{miller2025iot,mowla2023iot}. For the proposed project, a clear API and database model can reduce this problem within the project scope.

The fifth limitation is AI complexity. Some AI-based technologies may use computer vision, deep learning, or complex prediction models. Although these methods may be effective for specific tasks, they require appropriate datasets, rigorous training, validation, and sufficient computing resources. Siddiquee et al. use image processing for crop-related tasks, while Raju et al. introduce deep learning in a hydroponic setup \cite{siddiquee2022algorithms,raju2022hydroponics}. These examples show that advanced AI is possible, but it should not be added to a project without the required data and validation.

The sixth limitation is that many existing solutions are task-specific. Some systems focus on irrigation, others on hydroponics, crop detection, disease recognition, or actuator control. This specialization is useful for research, but it may not satisfy the needs of small-scale users who need an integrated and understandable monitoring platform. The proposed system is positioned in this gap by combining plant profiles, sensor data, dashboards, alerts, recommendations, and hybrid AI-supported classification in one web application.

The seventh limitation is the risk of overclaiming system capabilities. Environmental monitoring systems can detect abnormal environmental conditions, but they cannot automatically diagnose all plant diseases or guarantee plant recovery. Disease detection systems require image data, agronomic validation, or specialized models. The proposed system avoids this limitation by clearly stating that it does not diagnose plant diseases, does not use computer vision, and does not claim industrial-grade automation.

The eighth limitation is user understanding. Even if a system collects accurate data, users may not know how to interpret it. This is especially relevant for plant owners and educational users. For this reason, the proposed system includes explanations, risk factors, health score, condition status, and recommendations. This design follows the literature direction that smart agriculture should support decision-making rather than only collect data \cite{ahmed2024smart,mansoor2025integration}.

Overall, the limitations of existing solutions justify the design of the proposed system as an accessible, integrated, and transparent software solution. The system should not attempt to solve every smart agriculture problem. Instead, it should address a specific gap: environmental plant condition monitoring with IoT data collection, dashboard visualization, alert lifecycle management, and hybrid AI-supported recommendations for small-scale and educational scenarios.
\section{Requirements for the Proposed System}

Based on the literature review and comparative analysis, the proposed system should be designed as an integrated AI-IoT web-based monitoring platform. The main aim of such a system is to collect environmental sensor data, store and validate the collected data, analyze plant condition, generate alerts, and provide recommendations based on the monitored values. This requirement follows the general structure of IoT-based smart agriculture systems, where sensors, communication technologies, backend services, analytics, and user interfaces are combined to support monitoring and decision-making \cite{ahmed2024smart,mowla2023iot}.

The system should support user authentication, since plant profiles, sensor readings, alerts, and recommendations are associated with specific users. Secure access is also important because IoT monitoring systems may involve remote applications and user-related data. Huda et al. highlight authentication and secure access as relevant aspects of IoT-enabled plant monitoring systems \cite{huda2024iot}. Therefore, the proposed system should include role-based access control with separate roles for regular users and administrators.

The system should support plant profile management and sensor assignment. Plant profiles are necessary because sensor data should be connected to a specific monitored plant rather than treated as isolated numerical readings. Sensor assignment allows the system to associate incoming environmental data with the correct plant profile. This requirement follows the general logic of IoT-based monitoring systems, where physical sensor nodes or data sources must be connected with digital software platforms \cite{mowla2023iot}.

The system should collect environmental data related to plant condition monitoring. The selected parameters should include soil moisture, air temperature, air humidity, and light intensity. Such indicators are practical for small-scale monitoring systems and are consistent with parameters commonly used in IoT plant monitoring and smart irrigation systems \cite{huda2024iot,gaitan2025integrating}. Light intensity is also relevant because the light environment is recognized as an important group of indicators in smart agriculture monitoring \cite{huda2024iot,mowla2023iot}.

The system should validate and store sensor readings before using them for visualization or analysis. Validation is necessary because sensor data may be incomplete, delayed, or affected by measurement errors. The literature identifies data quality and implementation gaps as important challenges for smart sensing technologies \cite{miller2025iot}. Therefore, the proposed system should treat sensor readings as decision-support data and not as final agronomic evidence.

The system should provide dashboard visualization for current and historical environmental values. Visualization is necessary because raw sensor readings may be difficult for plant owners to interpret without context. In the literature on smart agriculture, real-time monitoring, data visualization, and data-based decision-making are identified as important components of IoT-based systems \cite{ahmed2024smart,mowla2023iot}. Therefore, environmental indicators, alerts, and recommendations should be presented in an understandable form on the dashboard.

The system should include plant condition assessment and machine learning-supported classification. The rule-based layer should provide transparent condition assessment, while the machine learning layer should support classification based on environmental sensor data and trend features. This requirement is consistent with the literature where AI and machine learning are used to support decision-making in agriculture, while practical limitations such as data quality, interpretability, and deployment context must be considered \cite{miller2025iot,nawaz2025iot}.

The system should generate alerts when abnormal environmental conditions are detected or when plant condition assessment indicates that user attention is required. Alerts are important because users may not continuously monitor the dashboard. In addition, the system should support recommendation logic, where recommendations are connected to detected risk factors. These recommendations should be presented as decision support, not as guaranteed plant recovery or expert agronomic advice.

The system should provide administrative monitoring and basic logging. Administrative functionality is needed to support structured management of users, plants, sensors, alerts, and system events. Logging supports traceability, debugging, and system maintenance. Since IoT systems include multiple data sources and user interactions, structured system monitoring can enhance reliability and maintainability \cite{mowla2023iot,huda2024iot}.

The system should also have clearly defined scope limitations. The proposed diploma project focuses on environmental condition monitoring, alert generation, and recommendation support based on soil moisture, air temperature, air humidity, and light intensity. Plant disease identification, computer vision, deep learning, industrial greenhouse automation, and actuator-based control are not considered in the current implementation. These areas could be investigated in further research; however, they would require different hardware, datasets, and validation methods \cite{siddiquee2022algorithms,raju2022hydroponics,ali2024automated}.

Finally, the system should be testable. The implemented modules should be evaluated through functional testing, API testing, database testing, and AI module verification. Testing is necessary to demonstrate that the developed system works according to the defined requirements. AI verification should be reported cautiously and should not be presented as real-world agronomic validation if the dataset is synthetic or controlled.

In summary, the proposed system requirements are derived from the literature and from the identified limitations of existing solutions. The system should provide an accessible AI-IoT monitoring platform for environmental plant condition monitoring, decision support, alerts, recommendations, and administrative management. This scope is realistic for the present diploma project and consistent with the literature on smart agriculture, IoT monitoring, and AI-supported decision-making \cite{ahmed2024smart,huda2024iot,mowla2023iot,miller2025iot,nawaz2025iot}.
